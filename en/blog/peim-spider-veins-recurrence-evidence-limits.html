Can spider veins return after treatment? What the evidence measures

The answer depends on what was treated, how a study defined “recurrence,” and how long participants were followed. Sclerotherapy studies do not establish outcomes for a PEIM procedure whose product and technique have not been confirmed.

“Return” can describe different situations

A previously treated vessel may become visible again; other vessels may appear over time; or new fine vessels may develop near a treated area. Unless a study defines its outcome, a measure called “recurrence” may not distinguish all of these situations. It also matters whether researchers assessed photographs, patient reports, or a vascular examination.

So ask what a promise of a permanent result or a single recurrence percentage actually means: in whom was it measured, after which procedure, and over what period?

A major review found an important evidence gap

A 2021 Cochrane review included 35 randomized trials and 3,632 participants comparing options for leg telangiectasias and reticular veins. The studies were heterogeneous, and the review reported that none of the included trials assessed recurrence or time to resolution. The evidence therefore cannot reliably estimate how many people will see vessels again after each option.

This describes what the included studies measured through March 2021. It does not prove recurrence is inevitable, and it does not answer every question about research or procedures published later.

A newer study still applies only to the technique it examined

A prospective cohort published in 2026 followed two groups for about three years after one session of sclerotherapy: 43 people with telangiectasias and 35 with reticular veins. The authors reported frequent recurrence in the telangiectasia group and minimal recurrence in the reticular-vein group. The study compared specific hypertonic-glucose solutions, with or without diluted polidocanol.

This follow-up is relevant, but a sclerotherapy cohort is not an estimate for every procedure. The participants, vessel type, substances, and technique matter. The study did not assess the product, route, or protocol used for this clinic’s PEIM service; ask the practitioner to clarify those details.

What to agree on before deciding

  • Which vessel or change is being assessed, and what is the proposed goal?
  • Which technique, product, and route are planned? “PEIM” alone does not answer this.
  • How does the team define improvement, persistence, and recurrence?
  • When will the area be reviewed, and what happens if some vessels remain visible?
  • Which signs call for contacting the team or seeking medical assessment?

Sudden swelling in one leg, significant pain, warm redness, or shortness of breath should not be treated as a cosmetic recurrence question; seek appropriate medical care. Photographs and an informational page cannot replace an examination when one is needed.

References and limits

  • Cochrane, 2021: treatments for telangiectasias and reticular veins — the included trials did not assess recurrence or time to resolution.
  • Prospective three-year follow-up cohort after sclerotherapy, published 2026 — two vessel groups and specific solutions; not a study of local PEIM.

The cited sclerotherapy studies do not establish equivalence with PEIM. Confirm the clinic’s product, protocol, and indication during an assessment.
