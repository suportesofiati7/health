“Nonsurgical blepharoplasty” can blur two different options

Plasma exeresis creates superficial thermal points in the skin. Surgical blepharoplasty is an operation that may remove skin and, depending on the assessment, address deeper structures. The nickname “nonsurgical blepharoplasty” does not mean the techniques do the same thing, address the same degree of excess tissue or have the same results and risks.

The local plasma service page does not confirm eyelid treatment. Do not infer an indication, experience or availability for this area from the service name or published studies.

First identify what is causing the eyelid change

Excess skin, brow position, eyelid droop (ptosis), fat pockets and asymmetry can contribute to similar appearances, but they are different findings. If you have visual heaviness, trouble seeing, eye irritation or a change in eyelid position, the question is not only cosmetic: seek an appropriate medical assessment, including ophthalmology when indicated.

A 2026 clinical review describes blepharoplasty as a reference treatment for more substantial tissue excess, fat prolapse or functional impairment. Choice depends on anatomy and goals; an educational page cannot diagnose the cause.

What plasma exeresis studies actually tested

A pilot study assessed ten people who had three interventions, with photographs and confocal microscopy taken at baseline and four to six weeks after the last session. Another clinical trial included 40 women, also with three monthly sessions, and assessed outcomes three months later. These studies observed changes in specific scales or measurements, but did not robustly compare the technique with surgery in a controlled trial.

A Brazilian observational study asked 16 people to complete questionnaires on days 7 and 30. Eyelid swelling and itching were among reported symptoms; reported satisfaction decreased during follow-up, and willingness to repeat did not support a promise of predictable results. Small samples and short follow-up cannot establish duration, long-term safety or which option is best for an individual.

So “nonsurgical” should not be read as “no recovery,” “no risks” or a substitute for surgery when excess tissue is substantial or affects function.

Questions for an informed decision

  • Which structure explains my concern: skin, brow, eyelid position, volume or more than one factor?
  • Are there eye or vision symptoms that need medical assessment before discussing an aesthetic procedure?
  • Is the proposed procedure performed on the eyelid? Which device, authorized purpose and instructions apply?
  • What evidence supports this device for my goal, and how does it compare with alternatives?
  • What are the recovery, risks, chance of partial response and plan if I dislike the result?
  • Would it be appropriate to speak with an ophthalmologist or oculoplastic surgeon before deciding?

Ask the practitioner to distinguish plasma exeresis evidence from evidence about other devices called plasma. If excess eyelid skin affects vision or you have a new eye symptom, prioritize medical assessment.

Sources and limitations

  • Rossi et al. (2018): pilot study of ten participants, three interventions and short follow-up.
  • Clinical trial of plasma exeresis in 40 women; the protocol and outcomes are not a comparison with surgery.
  • Ferreira et al. (2021): Brazilian observational study with 16 patients and questionnaires on days 7 and 30.
  • Martucci et al. (2026): review of periocular assessment and limits of nonsurgical options.
