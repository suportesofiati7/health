After vein treatment: brown marks or new fine vessels?

A brown mark and a network of fine red vessels are different outcomes in sclerotherapy studies. Appearance alone does not confirm the cause — and evidence for that technique does not establish local PEIM outcomes.

Brown pigmentation is not the same as a new fine vessel

After sclerotherapy, research describes post-sclerotherapy hyperpigmentation as brown discoloration over or around treated vessels. A network of very fine red vessels may instead be called telangiectatic matting. These terms describe appearances observed in specific contexts; they do not diagnose an individual mark from a photograph.

In a histopathology study of 19 people with persistent pigmentation after sclerotherapy, researchers found hemosiderin in the samples and altered melanin in some of them. This small study helps show that post-sclerotherapy marks may involve different pigments; it does not mean every brown mark has the same cause or needs the same care.

What studies call matting

A Brazilian case report describes telangiectatic matting as the appearance of vessels smaller than 0.2 mm after sclerotherapy. It is a technical term for a vascular observation, not a diagnosis a patient can make or a definitive explanation for every vessel that appears after a procedure.

Do not automatically equate matting with recurrence of a treated vessel. Recurrence, persistence of a vessel, and fine vessels appearing nearby can be measured differently. If you notice a change, describe it to the care team instead of trying to classify it yourself.

A recent follow-up study assessed pigmentation and matting separately

A prospective, blinded study followed people for about three years after one session of sclerotherapy: 43 participants with telangiectasias and 35 with reticular veins, using specific glucose solutions with or without polidocanol. The authors reported that pigmentation generally diminished over time and that matting persisted more in the telangiectasia group than in the reticular-vein group.

The findings apply to those vessel types, solutions and protocol. The study did not assess PEIM or confirm the clinic’s product, route or method. Do not use its timelines or results to predict the course of a local appointment.

What to note and ask at follow-up

  • When the change appeared and whether it is increasing, fading or staying the same.
  • Whether it looks like a brown area, a spot, a line or several fine vessels; take photographs only if the team recommends them and explains how to protect your privacy.
  • Which technique and product were used and what appearance was expected for that procedure.
  • How the team distinguishes pigmentation, a persistent vessel, matting and other causes during assessment.
  • When to return and which signs call for contacting the team sooner.

Severe pain, discharge, progressive worsening, fever, extensive blisters or a major color change should be reported according to the service’s public guidance; some signs may need medical assessment. Do not apply lightening products, acids or additional procedures to the area without discussing it with the responsible team.

References and limits

  • Three-year follow-up after sclerotherapy of telangiectasias and reticular veins — assessed pigmentation and matting in different vessel groups; it did not study PEIM.
  • Histopathology study of pigmentation after sclerotherapy — 20 biopsies from 19 people with persistent post-sclerotherapy pigmentation.
  • Brazilian case report on telangiectatic matting after sclerotherapy — defines the term and describes one case; it cannot estimate frequency or recommend a general treatment.

The cited studies concern sclerotherapy. The local service describes PEIM as an injectable procedure but does not publicly identify its product and technique; the practitioner should clarify whether the methods are related or different before applying any finding to an individual case.
