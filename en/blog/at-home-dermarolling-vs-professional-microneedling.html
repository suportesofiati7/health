At-home dermarolling and professional microneedling: differences and risks

Products sold for home use are not necessarily equivalent to professional procedures. Compare purpose, penetration, risks and safety questions.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational clinical reading

01 · “Dermaroller” does not identify depth or purpose

Similar-looking products can have different uses

Dermaroller is a common name for rollers with microneedles, but products vary: some only touch the most superficial layer, while others may penetrate the skin. A home product and a professional procedure should not be treated as equivalent merely because both use needles. The device, needle length and design, stated purpose and method of use matter—and cannot be inferred from photos or marketing.

02 · Infection, scarring and pigment changes are risks to consider

Puncturing the skin changes the risk profile

When skin is punctured, inadequate hygiene, improper technique or use on an unassessed condition can create risks, including infection, scarring and persistent pigment changes. The American Academy of Dermatology discourages home dermarolling that punctures the skin because of these risks. Do not share devices, try to reach a particular depth, or apply medicines or cosmetics through microchannels on your own.

03 · FDA guidance is US-specific, not Brazilian regulation

Regulation depends on the product and country

The FDA says it has not authorised microneedling devices as over-the-counter products for home use in the United States and has not assessed the safety or effectiveness of combining these devices with products applied to the skin. This is specific to the US regulatory system: it does not determine Brazilian product regularisation or local professional scope. For a particular device in Brazil, its regulatory status, manufacturer instructions and applicable professional rules need to be checked.

04 · Confirm technique, training and products used

Questions to ask before a procedure

Before a procedure, ask which device will be used, how its intended purpose and regulatory status can be confirmed, what training the operator has, how hygiene and cartridges are handled, which products will be applied, and which risks, alternatives and aftercare have been discussed. The practitioner must confirm the actual protocol and applicable authorisation. Read about microneedling; that page does not imply that a home roller is a safe or equivalent alternative.
