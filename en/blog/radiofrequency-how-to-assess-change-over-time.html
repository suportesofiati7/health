Radiofrequency: how should changes be assessed over time?

A mirror impression, a photograph, a practitioner’s assessment and a clinical scale can record different things. Radiofrequency studies use their own methods and timelines; knowing those methods helps you ask questions, but cannot predict another device’s or person’s result.

When discussing follow-up

Separate three questions

  1. What will be assessed, and on which dates?
  2. Who will review images or scales?

“Improvement” can mean different things

A practitioner-rated scale may assess laxity or wrinkles in a standardized way. A photograph documents appearance under specific conditions. A person may report satisfaction, discomfort or a change noticed in daily life. These outcomes complement each other but are not interchangeable: a scale change does not guarantee that everyone perceives the same benefit, and satisfaction does not replace clinical assessment.

To compare timepoints, ask what goal was set, which sign will be observed, when and by whom. If photographs are used, consistent position, distance, lighting, expression and angle help reduce differences unrelated to treatment.

How RF studies followed outcomes

In a randomized split-face trial, 20 participants received monopolar radiofrequency on one side and microfocused ultrasound on the other. Researchers followed them for up to six months and used a standardized face-and-neck laxity scale along with participants’ global assessment. This describes that study and those devices; it does not prove equivalence across modalities in every context or set a clinic’s schedule.

A separate prospective, open-label, single-arm trial followed 40 participants after monopolar radiofrequency on days 30, 60 and 180. It used investigator global assessment and satisfaction questionnaires. The paper reports a specific protocol—including premedication and a particular device—so its figures and dates should not become a promise or instruction for another treatment.

Why a selfie does not reproduce a study outcome

Front or side lighting, camera distance, lens, head position, expression, makeup and time of day can change appearance. A research protocol may combine standardized photographs, evaluators, scales and predetermined timepoints. A casual image can help you discuss a perception, but it does not automatically reproduce that method or confirm causality.

You can ask whether the service offers photography, how images are stored and used, and what consent is required. You do not need to send personal images to this page or share photographs to receive general information.

Useful questions for your consultation

  • What change is the goal, and how will it be assessed?
  • Which measure will be used: a clinical scale, photograph, personal report or a combination?
  • What are the follow-up timepoints, and why were they chosen?
  • What is the device model and modality? Does cited evidence apply to it?
  • What would count as an insufficient or variable response, or a reason to revisit the plan?

The public service page identifies radiofrequency but does not state every technical detail of the device or follow-up protocol. The practitioner should clarify those points; studies of other devices do not establish them.
