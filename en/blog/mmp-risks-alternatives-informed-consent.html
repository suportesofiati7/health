MMP: questions about risks, alternatives and informed consent

Informed consent means understanding the proposed goal, which product and route will be used, what the evidence actually shows, and what alternatives exist—including not having the procedure.

Why does the name MMP not answer everything?

MMP describes a technique for microinfusion of medication into the skin, but the name alone does not identify the substance, concentration, indication, depth, device or who may perform each step. Those details change how benefits and risks should be interpreted. The local service page does not specify a medication protocol; this article does not assume that any particular drug is used.

A recent narrative review collected small, heterogeneous studies of microinfusion in different types of alopecia. Results for one indication and product do not establish benefit for another condition, substance or protocol, nor do they show that the procedure is appropriate for everyone with hair loss.

What should an informed-consent conversation cover?

Ask for the proposal to be explained in clear language before deciding. The discussion can cover the goal, options, uncertainties, known risks and follow-up plan. Consider bringing these questions and asking for time to review product and procedure information.

  • Which condition is being considered, and what assessment supports that hypothesis?
  • What are the product name, manufacturer, concentration, batch and expiry date? What is its regulatory category and status with Brazil's Anvisa?
  • Is the product authorized for this purpose and route? Do its label and instructions support the proposed application?
  • Which studies involve the same substance, route, condition and outcome? What remains unknown?
  • Which local or systemic reactions are known, how would they be recognized, and whom should I contact?
  • Which other care options, treatments with evidence for the condition, or option to wait have been considered?
  • How will response be assessed, when will the plan be reviewed, and what would change or stop it?

The product and route matter

Brazil's Anvisa warns that cosmetics are intended for external use and must not be introduced into the skin through invasive techniques such as injection or microneedling. When a substance is administered across the skin barrier, the product's regulatory category and instructions need to match the proposed use. A brand name, advertising claim or phrase such as “for aesthetics” does not replace that check.

Ask to see the packaging, product identification and applicable traceability information. If it is unclear what will be applied or the documentation cannot be explained, postpone the decision until you have answers. This is general information; it does not determine the professional scope or regulatory status of any local protocol.

Risks are not limited to local irritation

Published studies are not large enough to rule out uncommon events or precisely estimate risk for each product and indication. The literature includes local effects and reports that raise the possibility of systemic absorption of medicines. This cannot predict an individual's risk, but it does make it inappropriate to assume that a substance applied through the skin can have no effects beyond it. Tell the responsible professional and, when relevant, the prescribing clinician about health conditions, allergies, medicines and ongoing treatment.

Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine for a procedure without speaking with its prescriber. If you have already had a session and are concerned about a symptom, seek clinical advice and share the product name, date and treated area. Severe or rapidly worsening symptoms need appropriate medical attention.

Alternatives include choosing not to proceed

Alternatives depend on the condition being assessed. For hair loss or thinning, an evaluation may distinguish diagnoses that have their own treatments; cosmetic measures may change appearance without treating the cause. An informed decision may also mean seeking another opinion, continuing the assessment or not starting a procedure while questions about the product, benefit or safety remain.

Sources and limits

  • Anvisa: cosmetics for aesthetic treatments and warning about invasive application of cosmetic products.
  • 2025 narrative review of medication microinfusion into skin; 14 studies with heterogeneous indications and limitations.
  • Case report of transient transaminase elevation after one MMP session with methotrexate; a single case cannot estimate frequency or risk for other products.

This material does not replace diagnosis, product labeling, instructions for use, procedure-specific consent or professional assessment. Confirm the clinic's product, indication, technique and protocol directly.
