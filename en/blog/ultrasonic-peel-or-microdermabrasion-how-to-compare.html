The names describe different methods

Microdermabrasion is a superficial mechanical exfoliation method that may use abrasion and suction; the local ultrasonic-peel service describes a vibrating spatula. A service name alone does not identify the device model, mechanism, intensity, indication, or protocol. A useful comparison therefore starts with what will be done to the skin, not with which name sounds newer.

These procedures should also not be confused with medical dermabrasion, microfocused ultrasound, or a sonic cleansing brush. Similar words can refer to technologies with different goals, depths, and evidence.

What has been studied about microdermabrasion

A 2006 clinical study evaluated repeated microdermabrasion for photoaging and the compatibility of cleansers and moisturizers with the procedure. An earlier histology study followed ten volunteers through six sessions and observed epidermal and dermal changes. These are small studies tied to the protocols investigated; they do not show that every device or plan produces the same result.

They help explain why microdermabrasion has its own literature. They do not answer whether the local ultrasonic spatula improves pores, marks, acne, scars, or hydration.

Research on one technology does not prove results for another

A 2021 Brazilian study compared manual cleansing with cleansing assisted by an electric sonic device in only 12 people, measuring skin parameters for up to 90 minutes. It studied cleansing with a sonic device; it was not a trial of ultrasonic peeling with a spatula or of microdermabrasion. Results from brushes, microfocused ultrasound, or other devices cannot automatically be applied to the spatula described by this service.

The cited sources do not directly compare ultrasonic peeling with microdermabrasion. They therefore do not support a claim that one is more effective, gentler, or suitable for more skin types than the other.

Questions that make the comparison useful

  • Which goal is being considered: surface cleansing, texture, another concern, or simply a smoother feel?
  • Which device, tip, and method will be used? What exactly touches the skin?
  • Which outcomes have been studied for that method and device, and over what timeframe?
  • How does the practitioner account for irritation, rosacea, inflamed acne, wounds, pigmentation, or recent procedures?
  • What does the technique not treat, and when might another approach be assessed?

A responsible answer should separate the local service description from published evidence about other devices and protocols. The choice depends on the goal, the skin’s condition, and the information available about the specific equipment.

References and limits

  • Abraham et al. Approaches to document the efficacy and safety of microdermabrasion procedure (2006) — clinical study of microdermabrasion and photoaging.
  • Andrews et al. The epidermal and dermal changes associated with microdermabrasion (2002) — ten volunteers and six sessions; sample and protocol limit generalization.
  • Aiello et al. Skin effect of facial cleansing combined with an electric sonic device (2021) — small cleansing study with a sonic device, not an ultrasonic spatula.

This comparison does not identify the clinic’s device or replace an individual assessment. The practitioner must confirm the local model, manual, intended use, and protocol before making device-specific claims.
