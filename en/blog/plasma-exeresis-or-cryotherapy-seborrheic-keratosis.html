Plasma exeresis or cryotherapy for seborrheic keratosis? What a trial assessed

A 2023 trial compared the methods for lesions already assessed as seborrheic keratoses. Its result does not choose a procedure for you: the study was small, followed participants for six weeks, and a later correction added an author’s relationship with the device manufacturer.

What was compared

A randomized clinical trial published in 2023 enrolled 35 men with lesions diagnosed as seborrheic keratoses. In each participant, one side was treated with plasma exeresis and the other with cryotherapy. The comparison involved one device, settings, participant selection and technique; it is not a test of every device called “plasma” or of every raised skin lesion.

At week three, 13 of 35 lesions (37.1%) treated with plasma and 6 of 35 (17.1%) treated with cryotherapy were clear; the difference did not reach statistical significance at that point (p=0.06). At week six, among lesions still followed, 16 of 28 (57.1%) in the plasma group and 6 of 29 (20.7%) in the cryotherapy group were clear (p=0.005). That measure favored plasma within this trial and timeframe; it does not predict results with another device, population or diagnosis.

Randomized trial published in 2023 (PubMed).

The later correction also matters

In December 2025, the journal published a correction. It added that one co-author was affiliated with the company that manufactured the device used in the study; the authors stated that the device was supplied for research only and that there were no financial relationships, funding or commercial interests that could have influenced the research. The correction also added ethics approval and data-availability statements. This does not automatically invalidate the results, but it is relevant information when assessing study design, transparency and potential interests.

Correction published in 2025 (PubMed).

What the trial cannot answer

  • It does not compare every plasma-exeresis or cryotherapy device and protocol.
  • It does not track recurrence, healing or long-term cosmetic outcomes; follow-up lasted six weeks.
  • It does not support treating a mole or other lesion without assessment: the comparison concerns diagnosed seborrheic keratoses.
  • It does not show that the clinic offers the device, settings or seborrheic-keratosis treatment described.
  • It does not replace a discussion of pigmentation, scarring, pain, aftercare and alternatives suited to the lesion and person.

How to use this information in a consultation

If someone proposes removing a lesion, first ask how it was assessed and whether medical diagnosis is needed before any destructive method. Then confirm the exact device name, modality, manufacturer’s intended use, who will perform the procedure and which alternatives and risks apply to your case. “Plasma” alone does not identify a technology or outcome.

This article recommends neither plasma exeresis nor cryotherapy, and does not confirm either procedure as a local service. The plasma technology service page explains the need for individual assessment; device, indication and professional-scope details should be verified with the responsible practitioner.
