Professional retinoic peels and at-home retinol: understand the differences

A professional peel and an at-home retinol product are not interchangeable based on their names. Learn what to compare and disclose before a procedure.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational clinical reading

01 · Similar names do not mean identical use

Retinoid is a family; retinol is one type

Retinoid is the name of a family of compounds; retinol is one type of retinoid found in skin-care products. “Retinoic peel” usually refers to a professional intervention, but the label alone does not confirm which substance, concentration, vehicle, contact time, sequence or aftercare will be used. Do not assume that your at-home product has the same formulation or that one can replace the other.

02 · Home products and professional peels have different contexts

Formulation and exposure change the question

An at-home product and a professional procedure differ in formulation, exposure, purpose and supervision. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that retinoids can make skin more sensitive and recommends discussing whether they are appropriate. Before a peel, the team needs to know which products you use, how often and whether you have had burning, redness or peeling. Do not stop, restart or change a prescribed medicine on your own to prepare.

03 · Experimental evidence is not a clinical protocol

One small study does not define every peel

A published laboratory study compared skin absorption of tretinoin in 0.05% cream with 5% chemical-peel formulas. The experiment shows that vehicle and formulation are part of exposure; it does not establish a concentration for the clinic’s service, equivalence between peels and cosmetics, a safe routine for everyone or the appropriate interval between uses. Evidence must match the product, indication and protocol being discussed.

04 · What to disclose and confirm at assessment

Share your routine before changing anything

Bring the product names or photographs of the labels you use and explain when you last used them, whether they are prescribed and how your skin reacted. Ask which professional formulation will be used, what evidence supports the proposed goal, whether your current routine changes the plan, which written instructions to follow and whom to contact if your skin reacts. Also read the listed retinoic peel service and the AAD guidance on preparing for a chemical peel. Guidance for your case depends on assessment and confirmation of the protocol.
