Red or blue spider veins: does appearance determine treatment?

The color visible through the skin is only part of the description. Vascular guidelines distinguish telangiectasias and reticular veins by caliber and location; a photo or shade alone cannot confirm which structure is involved or determine a procedure.

What the terms describe

In the CEAP classification used by vascular guidelines, telangiectasias are intradermal vessels smaller than 1 mm; reticular veins are subdermal vessels from 1 to less than 3 mm. Both may fall into clinical class C1. These measurements help professionals describe a finding; they are not a ruler for self-diagnosis.

People often describe small visible vessels as red, purple or blue. Because classification also considers caliber and anatomic plane, appearance or color in a photograph alone cannot establish whether a vessel is a telangiectasia, reticular vein or something else. This is a practical inference from the classification, not a color-based diagnostic rule.

Symptoms and history also matter

Share any pain, heaviness, itching, burning, swelling, skin changes or recent appearance of vessels. Mention a personal history of thrombosis or venous disease, pregnancy, medicines and previous treatments. These details do not by themselves determine whether an ultrasound or other examination is needed; they help the team understand the clinical question.

Sudden swelling in one leg, especially with pain, warmth or redness, should not be treated as a cosmetic concern: seek urgent medical assessment. Do not classify a vein from a photo or select a procedure based on color alone.

What sclerotherapy guidelines say — and do not say about PEIM

2023 guidelines from the Society for Vascular Surgery, American Venous Forum and American Vein and Lymphatic Society recommend liquid or foam sclerotherapy for symptomatic telangiectasias and reticular veins, and describe specific situations where transcutaneous laser may be considered. These recommendations address sclerotherapy and the clinical indications described in that guideline.

They do not establish that PEIM is synonymous with sclerotherapy, identify the product used at this clinic, or determine professional scope or local protocol. The goal, substance, route, regulatory status and rationale for a proposed treatment need to be explained and confirmed by the professional.

Questions to take to an assessment

  • Which vascular structure did the professional observe, and how was it classified?
  • Is the proposal cosmetic or related to symptoms? What other causes were considered?
  • Which product and route would be used, and what evidence applies specifically to that proposal?
  • What changes, alternatives, possible effects and follow-up plan should I understand before deciding?
  • Does any symptom or history call for medical or vascular assessment before a procedure?

Suitability depends on an in-person assessment and the exact proposed method. This page does not diagnose venous disease, recommend PEIM or replace medical assessment.
