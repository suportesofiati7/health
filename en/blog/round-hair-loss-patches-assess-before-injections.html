A localized patch is a clue, not a diagnosis

Alopecia areata is a condition that can cause one or more round areas of hair loss, but other conditions can also produce localized patches. Looking at a patch in a photo or at home cannot confirm the cause. Assessment may include examining the scalp and hairs, asking about personal and family history and, depending on findings, dermoscopy, selected laboratory tests or a biopsy.

The American Academy of Dermatology advises dermatologists to assess hair-loss areas, scalp, nails and health history when investigating alopecia areata. Which tests are needed depends on the findings; this does not mean everyone needs a biopsy or a panel of lab tests.

Why the pattern changes the mesotherapy discussion

Published reviews of mesotherapy for hair loss mainly study pattern hair loss, including androgenetic alopecia, and describe heterogeneous substances, protocols and outcomes. A recent systematic review concluded that standardized regimens and larger controlled studies are lacking. These data do not show that mesotherapy treats alopecia areata and cannot be transferred across diagnoses or products.

The local service page does not identify a target diagnosis, injected substance or indication for patchy hair loss. MMP is also an application technique, not a diagnosis. The cause must be clarified by an appropriately qualified professional before assessing whether any cosmetic procedure belongs in a plan.

What to bring to an assessment

  • When you first noticed the patch, whether it appeared suddenly and how it has changed.
  • Dated photographs, if available; they do not replace an in-person examination.
  • History of similar hair loss, health conditions, medicines and treatments already tried.
  • Any patches in the eyebrows, beard or other areas, and changes in the nails.
  • Itching, scale, pain, redness, sores or discharge on the scalp.

Do not start injections or stop prescribed medicines to try to control hair loss without assessment. Seek prompt medical assessment if loss is spreading rapidly or there is pain, inflammation, discharge or scar-like skin.

Questions to ask before any procedure

  • Which diagnosis or possibility could explain this pattern, and who should confirm it?
  • What evidence supports the proposed substance and route for this specific diagnosis?
  • Is the product, its regulatory status and its indication suitable for this use?
  • Which medical alternatives or the option of no procedure should be considered?
  • How will diagnosis and outcomes be monitored without promising hair growth?

Injections used by dermatologists for some conditions, such as intralesional corticosteroids for selected alopecia areata cases, are medical treatments with their own indications. They are not equivalent to cosmetic mesotherapy and are not automatically appropriate for you.

References and limits

  • American Academy of Dermatology: alopecia areata diagnosis and treatment — public information on dermatologic assessment and medical options; not individual advice.
  • Gupta et al., systematic review of mesotherapy for hair loss (2023) — 27 articles, varied agents and lack of standardized regimens; it does not establish efficacy for alopecia areata or a local product.
  • Rinaldi et al., systematic review of mesotherapy in pattern hair loss (2022) — evidence focused mainly on pattern hair loss, with sample and comparison limitations.
