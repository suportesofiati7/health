TCA or Jessner peel: what differs and what should be confirmed?

TCA and Jessner solution are different chemical agents used in distinct approaches — and sometimes in combination in the literature. A useful comparison starts with the actual formula and plan, not a promise about which is “stronger.”

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational guide; confirm exact formula and protocol

They are not two names for the same thing

The service page identifies ATA as trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Jessner solution is a combination of agents; traditional and modified formulations can differ. A trade or common name alone therefore does not reveal the formula, goal, or intended depth of a proposal.

Dermatology reviews describe TCA peels at different depths and Jessner solution as an agent used on its own or, in some published protocols, before TCA. That is why a generic comparison cannot predict a clinic’s plan.

What the literature can — and cannot — compare

Studies may evaluate a specific formula, sequence, concentration, population, and goal. A result for a Jessner–TCA combination does not show that every Jessner peel is combined with TCA, or that a service called TCA follows that protocol. It also does not establish universal superiority for acne, pigmentation, or photoaging.

“Deeper” or “stronger” does not automatically mean better. Intended depth, the concern, skin condition and tone, history of pigmentation and healing, and expected recovery all belong in an individual professional decision.

Compare the plan, not just the label

Before consenting, ask the practitioner to explain in plain language:

  • which agent and formulation are proposed, including whether they are combined;
  • which specific concern the plan addresses and how it was assessed;
  • the intended depth, relevant risks considered, and how recovery may vary;
  • what alternatives exist and why they may or may not fit your goal;
  • which written instructions and contact channel will be provided afterward.

This guide does not provide a recipe, concentration, application layers, preparation, or a recommendation to choose one agent. Those details depend on the product, protocol, and in-person assessment.

Discuss pigmentation history individually

Tell the practitioner if your skin tends to leave marks after acne or irritation, if you have had a recent procedure, active inflammation, which medicines and products you use, or a history of altered healing. This cannot predict your response by itself, but it helps the practitioner discuss risks and alternatives. Do not stop medicines or start active products on your own before a peel.
