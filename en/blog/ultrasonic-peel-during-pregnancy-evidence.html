Is there specific evidence for an ultrasonic spatula during pregnancy?

I found no clinical studies establishing the safety of aesthetic ultrasonic-spatula peeling during pregnancy. The absence of reported harm does not show that the procedure has been studied enough to be considered safe. The local service description refers to a vibrating spatula applied to the skin, but does not identify the manufacturer, model or device instructions.

“Ultrasound” can also refer to very different technologies. A surface-cleansing spatula is not automatically equivalent to diagnostic ultrasound, therapeutic ultrasound or high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU). Evidence and contraindications for one modality should not be transferred to another.

What reviews of cosmetic procedures say

A systematic review of cosmetic procedures in pregnancy found a lack of controlled studies and sparse data for many interventions. A 2026 clinical review also notes a lack of pregnancy-specific data for ultrasound technologies, but mainly discusses deeper energy modalities such as HIFU. That does not directly answer the safety of a surface spatula—or prove it is safe because it is described for a different purpose and applied at the skin surface.

Because ultrasonic peeling is elective, a cautious approach during pregnancy is to postpone it until the practitioner can check the exact device manual and discuss the question with the prenatal care team. This caution reflects the lack of specific evidence, not proof that the spatula has caused fetal harm.

If you had a session before learning you were pregnant

Do not use HIFU studies or reports about other technologies to calculate the risk of an individual exposure. Note the date and treatment area; if possible, ask the clinic for the manufacturer and model, instructions for use, and share those details with your prenatal care team. Available sources do not allow harm to be inferred from one session, and this article cannot recommend a universal test or course of action.

If you notice pain, persistent irritation or another skin change, contact the practitioner who performed the procedure and your prenatal team. Do not start medicines or new products on your own.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding are different situations

A recommendation to postpone an elective cosmetic procedure during pregnancy does not automatically determine what to do while breastfeeding. The 2026 review emphasizes that these situations need separate assessment, considering the modality, products used, treatment area and potential skin contact with the baby. Ask for the exact device instructions and practitioner guidance before resuming any session.

Questions to ask the practitioner

  • What are the brand, model and stated purpose of the device?
  • Do current instructions mention pregnancy or breastfeeding? Can I review the relevant section?
  • Is the procedure strictly a surface spatula, or does it include another energy mode, current or product?
  • How would the recommendation differ during breastfeeding, and what should be confirmed with prenatal care?
  • If a session already occurred, what device details can help the obstetric team give advice without extrapolating from another technology?

The service page does not publish a model, frequency, instructions or pregnancy recommendation. Confirm those details with the practitioner; this guide does not replace obstetric care.

Sources and limitations

  • Trivedi, Kroumpouzos and Murase (2017): review of cosmetic procedures during pregnancy and lactation; notes the lack of controlled studies.
  • Clinical review of dermatologic and cosmetic procedures during pregnancy.
  • Kovacs et al. (2026): narrative review of pregnancy, lactation and cosmetic procedures; discusses gaps in energy-based technologies, including high-intensity ultrasound.

These sources do not directly study the local ultrasonic-spatula model or establish a protocol for this clinic.
