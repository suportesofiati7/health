Aesthetic plasma or CO₂ laser: why the name is not enough to compare

“Aesthetic plasma” can refer to different modalities, and CO₂ lasers also vary by delivery mode. Without identifying the exact device and purpose, comparing results or choosing between them creates an equivalence the sources do not support.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational clinical reading

“Plasma” does not identify one technology

The term may refer to cold atmospheric plasma, thermal modalities such as exeresis, or other systems that generate energy in different ways. Reviews of cold atmospheric plasma include different devices, designs, and indications; studies of one modality do not establish the effectiveness of another. The public service page does not confirm here the exact model, handpiece, or modality offered.

What changes when the term is CO₂ laser

CO₂ identifies an ablative laser source used in resurfacing, but the plan still depends on the device and delivery mode. Reviews of fractional CO₂, for example, describe their own literature and do not automatically validate a plasma protocol. The AcuPulse service page describes an ablative laser, while the configuration and parameters for an individual case need to be explained by the practitioner.

So “plasma versus CO₂” is not a direct comparison between two versions of the same procedure. Target tissue, physical interaction, studied indication, area, and recovery may differ.

Do not compare results without comparing the question

A cold-plasma study for an inflammatory condition or wound does not answer whether a thermal exeresis device improves aesthetic texture. Likewise, fractional CO₂ results for a specific indication do not predict results from another device, mode, or goal. A plausible mechanism, a pilot study, and a clinical outcome are different levels of evidence.

What to confirm during an assessment

  • Manufacturer, model, applicator, and exact modality — not only the word “plasma.”
  • The purpose described in the manual and the product’s Brazilian regularisation; use the Anvisa system with the exact identifier.
  • Which concern is being addressed, how it was assessed, and what evidence refers to the same device, mode, and indication.
  • Risks, recovery, follow-up, alternatives, and the scope of the person performing the procedure.
  • If the modality cannot be identified, pause the comparison until that information is available.

These questions support informed consent; they do not replace an assessment or establish that a device is irregular or unsuitable.
