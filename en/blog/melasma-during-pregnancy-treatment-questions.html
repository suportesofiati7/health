Pregnancy-related pigmentation may change over time

Melasma is one possible cause of brown patches on the face during pregnancy, but not every new mark is melasma. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) lists melasma among common skin changes during pregnancy and says the patches often fade after birth, although they may persist. A spot that grows, changes, bleeds or looks different from others needs clinical assessment rather than being treated as ordinary pigmentation.

Because appearance may change after pregnancy, the benefit of starting an elective cosmetic treatment now should be considered carefully. Delaying an intervention does not mean ignoring the skin; it can be a temporary choice while confirming the diagnosis and clarifying options that fit the pregnancy.

Why not start a brightening product or procedure on your own?

“Brightening product” is not a safety category. Products can contain different ingredients, concentrations and instructions; a cosmetic, a medicine and a professional procedure are not interchangeable. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) advises waiting until after pregnancy to treat melasma and recommends discussing over-the-counter products with a dermatologist, since their safety during pregnancy may not be easy to determine. This does not replace advice from the obstetric care team or mean that every ingredient has the same risk.

Peels and light-based technologies also depend on the modality, any associated product and the indication. General sources about melasma in pregnancy do not establish the safety of a specific professional protocol. Because this is an elective cosmetic concern, confirm the decision with a dermatologist and prenatal care team before booking or starting active treatment. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without speaking with its prescriber.

Sun protection is a different conversation from active treatment

ACOG recommends sunscreen and a wide-brimmed hat when outdoors to help prevent patches from darkening. Sun protection is a care measure, not a promise that melasma will disappear and not automatic approval to combine other active products. Ask for guidance suited to your routine and skin sensitivity, especially if your skin is irritated or your obstetric team has given specific advice.

Questions to take to an appointment

  • Has this mark been assessed and found consistent with melasma, or does it need another evaluation?
  • What may happen to the pigmentation after birth, and when should it be reviewed?
  • Which products am I using? Should any be reviewed with the prescriber or prenatal care team?
  • What sun-protection approach fits my routine and skin sensitivity?
  • Has the proposed procedure and modality been studied during pregnancy? Which evidence or manufacturer instructions support the decision?
  • If postponing is preferable, when and with which professional should I revisit the plan?

The local pigmentation service page does not publish a pregnancy protocol. This guide does not recommend an ingredient, concentration or procedure for an individual.

Sources and limits

  • ACOG: skin conditions during pregnancy, melasma and general sun-protection measures.
  • American Academy of Dermatology: melasma overview; advises waiting until after pregnancy to treat and discussing products.
  • UKTIS: pregnancy data on hydroquinone exposure are very limited; do not extend this document to other ingredients.

These sources do not replace prenatal or dermatology assessment and do not establish the safety of local formulations, products or procedures. Confirm recommendations with professionals familiar with your health situation.
