More hair after laser hair removal: what the evidence says

More hair after laser or light treatment can have different explanations. Learn what research on paradoxical hair growth says—and why it cannot predict your case.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational clinical reading

01 · Paradoxical growth is a specific clinical term

Describe a change in hair rather than self-diagnosing

Noticing more visible, coarser or more numerous hairs after sessions can be concerning, but the observation alone does not confirm a complication or explain its cause. “Paradoxical hypertrichosis” is an adverse effect discussed in the literature on laser and light hair removal. Changes can coincide with hormonal variation, natural growth or differences in observation; a webpage cannot distinguish these possibilities.

02 · A published estimate is not an individual prediction

The review found substantial variation across studies

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis included 22 studies and 9,733 participants. The pooled estimate was 3%, with a confidence interval of 1% to 6%, but between-study heterogeneity was very high. Cases were associated mainly with the face and neck and were uncommon outside those areas in the included studies. These figures are not a guaranteed rate for LightSheer, another platform, a Brazilian service or an individual.

03 · Area, device and study population matter

Facial findings do not automatically apply to the body

The studies included different devices, protocols and populations; the review found insufficient data to establish some associations, including the contributions of sex and skin phototype. A 2025 prospective study examined facial hair removal with alexandrite laser in a specific population and found a different prevalence, illustrating why its estimate should not be transferred to other devices or indications. Neither study identifies the clinic’s protocol.

04 · Document the change and request reassessment

Discuss the change before altering the plan

If you notice a persistent change, record where it occurred, when it began and how it differs from before, and share it with the practitioner. Ask whether the area needs reassessment, which explanations are plausible, and whether the plan should be adjusted, paused or continued. Do not change medicines or your schedule on your own. For general response factors, also read about facial hair and hormonal context and hair cycles and treatment intervals.
