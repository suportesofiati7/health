Hydroquinone and darkening patches: when to seek a review?

A darkening patch can have many explanations. A rare condition has been reported after topical hydroquinone use, but case reports cannot diagnose an individual or estimate how often it occurs.

When the original concern changes

If a pigmented area becomes darker, changes in appearance, or spreads while you are using a lightening product, arrange a review with the clinician who prescribed or recommended it. Do not increase the amount, frequency, or strength on your own, and do not add a peel or light-based procedure to “correct” the change before its cause is assessed.

Bring the product package or a clear photograph of its ingredients, plus the start date, frequency, duration, and any other products used on the area. These details help the clinician distinguish among possible explanations; this page cannot make that distinction for you.

What has been reported with hydroquinone

A systematic review collected 56 published reports describing 126 people with hydroquinone-associated exogenous ochronosis. The reports commonly described facial blue-black or gray-blue patches, often with a net-like pattern. The median reported duration of use was five years. Because the review was built from case reports and case series without a population denominator, it cannot tell us the incidence or a person's individual risk.

Concentrations were often unknown; some reports involved concentrations above 4%. The authors noted an association in the reported cases with higher concentrations and use longer than three months. This is not a safe-use threshold, a prediction, or permission to use a product for that duration. A rare complication is one possible explanation for worsening pigment—not a self-diagnosis.

Why assessment comes before another procedure

Exogenous ochronosis can resemble treatment-resistant melasma or other pigment changes, and reports describe difficult management. A Brazilian case report also documents the condition after topical hydroquinone. A clinician needs to review the history and skin findings and decide whether further investigation is needed before choosing treatment.

Do not assume that all darkening during treatment is ochronosis. Inflammation, sun exposure, the original diagnosis, product ingredients, and other conditions can also matter. The appropriate next step depends on the individual assessment.

Information to take to the appointment

  • The product name, ingredient list, concentration if stated, source, and whether it was prescribed.
  • When you started, how often you applied it, how long you used it, and where.
  • When the change began, how it looks now, and whether there was irritation or another recent treatment.
  • All other products or procedures used on the area, including peels, lasers, or light devices.
  • Ask whether to continue, pause, or change the product; do not alter a prescribed plan based only on an online article.

Sources and limits

  • Ishack and Lipner (2022), systematic review of hydroquinone-associated exogenous ochronosis: 56 reports and 126 cases.
  • Brazilian case report and literature review of exogenous ochronosis associated with hydroquinone.

Published cases do not establish incidence, a universal threshold, or the cause of a particular person's pigmentation. Product and clinic-specific decisions require clinician review.
