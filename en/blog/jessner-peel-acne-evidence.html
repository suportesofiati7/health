Jessner peel and acne: what do studies actually show?

Research has evaluated chemical peels for people with acne, including studies of Jessner solution. That does not mean every peel, formula or acne presentation has the same evidence or indication.

Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational information; indication requires professional assessment

“A peel for acne” does not identify one formula

Chemical peels use different agents and combinations. Jessner appears in the literature as a specific solution, but the name alone does not disclose the exact composition, preparation, intended depth, area, skin preparation or who may perform a particular intervention. It also cannot establish that the formula studied is the same option being discussed at a local consultation.

Acne can include blackheads and whiteheads, papules, pustules or nodules, and may coexist with flat marks or scars. A study that counts lesions in one group does not automatically answer all of these concerns.

What small studies can add

A 1999 randomised trial compared Jessner and glycolic acid on opposite sides of the face in 26 people. Both approaches improved acne scores after three study sessions, with no significant difference between sides; exfoliation was greater on the Jessner-treated side. That finding applies to that sample and those agents, not as a recommendation for an individual or clinic.

A 2020 randomised trial included 36 participants, most with Fitzpatrick skin types IV and V, and compared Jessner with salicylic acid for acne and post-acne hyperpigmentation. Measures improved on both sides and the main outcome did not differ significantly between groups. Burning, stinging and exfoliation were reported; post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation was reported once on the Jessner side. The study does not prove universal safety, equivalence across peels or applicability to every person with skin of colour.

Why this does not replace an acne plan

A systematic review of trials on peels for acne identified heterogeneous studies and limitations in evidence quality. Findings of improvement in some studies do not show that a peel alone is the best approach, nor that it replaces diagnostic assessment and established care options. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that dermatologists use peels for some types of acne; “some types” matters and does not automatically validate every product, provider or protocol.

Persistent, painful or nodular acne, acne that scars, or acne with emotional impact deserves appropriate health care discussion. An aesthetic procedure should not delay medical assessment when it is needed.

Questions to ask at an assessment

  • What is being assessed: active lesions, flat marks, scars or more than one concern?
  • Which product and composition are being considered, and what is the intended purpose?
  • Does the cited evidence match the formula, condition and skin profile being discussed?
  • How do medicines, irritation, sun exposure and a history of dark marks affect the assessment?
  • What alternatives exist, including addressing acne first or seeking dermatology assessment?
  • What risks, written care instructions and contact signs will be explained?

Do not use studies to choose products, combine acids or reproduce applications at home. The FDA warns that chemical peel products used without appropriate supervision can cause serious injuries.
