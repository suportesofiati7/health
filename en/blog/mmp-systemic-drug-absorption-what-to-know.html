“Local delivery” does not prove zero systemic absorption

MMP delivers medication through the skin using microneedles. Applying a substance to one area may concentrate some of it in the treated tissue, but that does not show that none crosses the skin or reaches the circulation. The amount delivered and absorbed depends on the medication, concentration, volume, area, depth, device and protocol.

These are also different questions: how much solution was deposited in the skin; how much remained locally; how much entered the body; and what clinical effect occurred. A study that weighs skin samples to estimate delivery does not, by itself, measure drug concentrations in blood or prove that systemic effects are impossible.

What MMP studies with methotrexate illustrate

A prospective controlled trial assessed 17 people with frontal fibrosing alopecia. Methotrexate was applied to one side of the scalp over three sessions, with the other side serving as a control. No laboratory changes were observed in that group and protocol. The authors nevertheless stated that the degree of systemic absorption was unknown. This result does not establish safety for every product, dose, treatment area or person.

In a separate case report, a woman with alopecia areata and lupus received one MMP session with 20 mg of methotrexate on the scalp. Her liver enzymes rose temporarily one week later and returned to baseline at the next check. One case does not prove MMP caused the change, estimate how often it happens or predict risk from other substances. It does show why “local” should not be described as synonymous with “no possible systemic effect.”

A 2026 narrative review summarized 14 MMP studies across different indications and medications and described the technique as supporting local deposition with low systemic absorption. That wording summarizes evidence that is still limited and heterogeneous; it is not an absorption measurement for every substance and does not remove the uncertainty raised by individual studies.

Questions to ask before consenting

  • What is the exact substance, concentration, total amount and proposed indication?
  • By which route will the product be used, and is that route included in the product’s documentation and authorized purpose?
  • Is there systemic-absorption evidence for this specific medication delivered by MMP with a comparable device, area and protocol?
  • Which health conditions, allergies and current medicines should be shared with the clinician responsible for prescribing and performing the procedure?
  • For this medication and person, is clinical follow-up or laboratory monitoring relevant? Who decides and how will follow-up occur?
  • What alternatives are available, including not having the procedure, and which uncertainties should be explained in consent?

Bring an up-to-date list of medicines and health conditions. Do not stop a prescription, change a dose or order tests on your own based on this article; those decisions depend on the medication and clinical assessment.

What this page cannot determine

The local service does not publish the substance, dose or protocol planned for each person. So this page cannot determine whether systemic absorption will occur, whether tests are needed or whether a particular medication is appropriate for MMP. Product registration also does not prove that every route or indication is authorized.

If the team says the effect is exclusively local, ask what evidence supports that statement for the exact product and protocol. If that information is unavailable, the uncertainty should be explained before a decision.

Sources and limitations

  • Arbache et al. (2019): experimental estimate of the amount delivered into human skin for one protocol; it did not measure blood absorption.
  • Controlled methotrexate MMP trial in frontal fibrosing alopecia: 17 participants; authors said systemic absorption was unknown.
  • Nogueira and Bonamigo (2023): report of temporary transaminase elevation after MMP with methotrexate in one patient; it cannot estimate incidence or general causality.
  • Nagshabandi et al. (2026): narrative review of 14 heterogeneous MMP studies; it does not replace absorption assessment for each medication.

The sources do not identify the substance or protocol that may be used locally. Confirm product, route, indication and follow-up with the practitioner.
