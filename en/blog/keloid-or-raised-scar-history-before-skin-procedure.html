Keloid or raised-scar history: what to share before a skin procedure

A raised scar after surgery, a piercing, acne or another injury is important information before a procedure. Describe what happened and where; the professional can assess the procedure, treatment area and expected skin injury without assuming one rule fits everyone.

What does it mean when a scar is “raised”?

Keloids and hypertrophic scars are both raised scars, but they are not the same and cannot be confirmed from a short description or photo. A scar that grew beyond the original injury, increased over time, itches or hurts should be described to the professional or assessed by a dermatologist. Do not try to diagnose your own scarring tendency.

Your personal history—and, when relevant, family history—may help the team decide whether a scar needs clarification before another procedure is proposed. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recommends telling the professional if you or family members develop keloids before cosmetic procedures.

What information can help?

  • What preceded the scar: acne, cut, burn, piercing, tattoo, surgery, injection or cosmetic procedure.
  • Its location, approximate size and whether it extended beyond the original injury.
  • Whether it grew, remained stable, itched, hurt or limited movement; when it began changing.
  • Previous treatments and how the scar responded.
  • Family history of raised scars and any diagnosis by a dermatologist.
  • Older and current photos, if available; do not delay assessment of an active or changing scar to obtain them.

Different procedures do not have one shared rule

Ablative laser, microneedling, chemical peels, microdermabrasion and plasma procedures differ in how they affect the skin, including depth and area. Advice for one device should not automatically be applied to another service. For example, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists a history of keloids as a factor that may make someone unsuitable for the microneedling devices it has evaluated. That guidance is specific to the scope and devices of the US agency, not a Brazilian rule for every procedure.

The AAD advises dermatology assessment before microdermabrasion for people who scar easily and recommends sharing a keloid history before cosmetic procedures. These recommendations support an individual discussion, not a conclusion that every procedure is prohibited or safe. The treatment area, purpose, equipment, instructions and prior healing need to be considered.

Questions for your consultation

  • Does this procedure injure the skin? At what depth and over what area?
  • Is there evidence or device guidance for someone with my scarring history?
  • Is the device model and intended use confirmed? Would the recommendation change if a scar is in the treatment area?
  • Should I have a dermatology assessment before deciding?
  • Are there alternatives with a different injury profile, or an option to wait?
  • Which changes after treatment should be reported, and who will follow up?

If the device, depth, protocol or follow-up plan is unclear, ask for clarification before deciding. The website does not publish a local protocol for a history of keloids.

Sources and limits

  • AAD: share personal or family keloid history before cosmetic procedures and discuss alternatives.
  • AAD: dermatology assessment before microdermabrasion for people who scar easily.
  • FDA: safety and selection information for microneedling devices authorized in the United States; not Brazilian regulation and not all techniques.

These sources do not identify local devices or protocols, determine Brazilian professional scope or replace assessment by a dermatologist or responsible professional.
