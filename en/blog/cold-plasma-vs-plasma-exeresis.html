Cold plasma and plasma exeresis: why they are different modalities

Cold atmospheric plasma and plasma exeresis may both be called “plasma,” but they are different modalities. Learn why their mechanisms, studies and protocols should not be conflated.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational clinical reading

01 · “Plasma” can name different technologies

The name alone does not explain how skin is affected

In dermatology, cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) usually refers to partially ionized gas produced near atmospheric pressure and under low-temperature conditions. CAP sources include plasma jets and dielectric-barrier discharges. “Plasma exeresis” describes a focal-discharge modality that can heat skin and create small thermal injury zones; one histological study observed this effect with a specific handheld device. “Plasma pen” is a broad commercial label and does not, by itself, identify a device’s physics or intended use. Confirm the model before applying evidence or claims.

02 · What cold-plasma research has studied

Cold jets and discharges appear in specific research contexts

A systematic review of CAP in dermatology found different sources, gases, application methods and populations. Many studies examined wounds and other medical conditions, not cosmetic procedures using any device called plasma. The review maps a heterogeneous research field; it does not establish a broad aesthetic indication or validate plasma exeresis.

03 · Plasma exeresis has a different thermal interaction

One device study does not represent every pen

In a small histological assessment of one specific handheld device, researchers described an electrostatic spark that heated skin and created focal microthermal wounds. A clinical trial of plasma exeresis, in turn, studied seborrheic keratoses in a defined group and protocol. Neither study proves outcomes, settings, safety or suitability for another model, concern or the service offered at this clinic.

04 · What to confirm during assessment

Identify the technology before discussing results

  • What are the exact manufacturer and model of the device?
  • Does it use cold atmospheric plasma, a focal thermal discharge or another modality?
  • Which manufacturer document describes its intended use and warnings?
  • What indication is being discussed, and which studies evaluated the same device type, condition and outcome?
  • How can I check applicable regulatory status, and who may operate the device?

Physical plasma should also not be confused with platelet-rich plasma (PRP), a blood-derived product. Read about the listed plasma technology service and the guide to plasma devices and PRP. The clinic’s available modality and protocol still require professional confirmation.
