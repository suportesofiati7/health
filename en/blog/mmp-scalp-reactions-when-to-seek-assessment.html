MMP on the scalp: when should a reaction be assessed?

A change that grows, hurts or forms nodules should be reported to the responsible team. A published case after repeated MMP sessions shows why new changes need assessment—but cannot establish that the technique caused the condition or predict another person’s risk.

What the studies do—and do not—show

Research on microinfusion of medication into the skin remains limited and includes different substances, indications and techniques. A 2025 narrative review described 14 MMP studies; mild reactions were reported in several reports, but this body of evidence does not establish a reliable event rate or a universal recovery timeline for every product and protocol.

In 2026, authors reported a patient who developed painful, itchy inflammatory scalp nodules after repeated sessions. The case involved specific clinical factors and products, as well as its own diagnostic assessment. The authors state that causality between the procedure and the condition cannot be established from one case. It does not show that MMP generally causes this problem; it is a reason to have persistent or progressive changes appropriately assessed.

Which changes should you report?

Ask the responsible professional in advance for written instructions about what may be expected with the specific protocol, when to get in touch and how follow-up works. Contact them if there is progressive worsening, increasing pain, swelling, new nodules, discharge, substantial crusting or a new area of hair loss. These signs cannot be diagnosed remotely and may have several causes, but they warrant assessment rather than self-treatment.

Seek urgent medical care if redness or swelling spreads, the area becomes very warm and painful, or you develop fever or significant illness. General signs of infection vary and can be less obvious across skin tones; if unsure, describe the symptoms to a health professional.

What to note before asking for advice

  • Session date and when the change began; note whether it appeared right away or days or weeks later.
  • Location, approximate size, and whether there is pain, itching, warmth, discharge or a change in hair density.
  • Photographs in similar lighting, without delaying assessment if symptoms are worsening.
  • Product name and batch if the clinic provided these details; do not guess the contents from the term MMP.
  • Medicines, health conditions and other recent scalp procedures.

Do not squeeze nodules, apply antibiotics or steroids on your own, or stop prescribed medication without advice from its prescriber. If a reaction occurs, postpone another session until you receive professional guidance.

The local plan needs confirmation

The service page does not specify the product, depth, device, interval between sessions or recovery instructions. These details may change what is expected and how symptoms should be handled. Ask who your clinical contact is, which changes need an in-person assessment and what product records will be available. This article does not replace the professional’s aftercare plan or a dermatology assessment.

Sources and limits

  • Oliveira et al. (2026): case report of inflammatory scalp nodules after repeated microinfusion sessions; one case cannot establish causality or frequency.
  • 2025 narrative review of MMP: 14 studies across different indications, with evidence limitations.
  • American Academy of Dermatology: increased redness, swelling, pain or pus may warrant contacting a health professional; general guidance, not an MMP protocol.

These sources do not establish risk or expected recovery for the service offered at this clinic. Confirm the device, product, indication and instructions with the responsible professional.
