Plasma exeresis and keloids: what can one pilot study tell us?

A pilot study evaluated plasma exeresis for keloids, but its sample, design and follow-up limit what can be concluded. Here are the questions that remain.

Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational evidence review; not an individual treatment recommendation.

01 · A small pilot is a starting point

What the researchers studied

The 2023 pilot study evaluated plasma exeresis for keloid scars. It included 16 people and 24 scars, with assessment before treatment and five months later. The paper reports changes in scar-scale measures after two or three sessions.

This is a small, single-arm study. It can describe what happened in this selected group, but without a comparison group it cannot show how much change was caused by the procedure rather than natural variation, measurement effects or other factors.

02 · Read the exclusions as carefully as the results

The safety finding has important limits

The paper reports no significant complications in the analyzed group, apart from temporary redness. However, its methods excluded people with severe complications such as infection, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or scarring, as well as people who did not complete follow-up. That makes the result unsuitable as proof that complications do not occur.

Five months of follow-up also cannot establish long-term recurrence or delayed effects. The study’s outcome should not be converted into a personal probability or a guarantee.

03 · The word “plasma” does not identify the local device

Do not transfer this study to a different modality

The pilot used a named plasma-exeresis device and a dermatologist-performed protocol. The local service page does not identify its manufacturer, model or physical modality. Evidence about this particular device cannot be assumed to describe cold atmospheric plasma, another plasma system or a service whose equipment has not been confirmed.

Keloids also need an individualized medical assessment. A study of selected scars does not establish that an energy procedure is appropriate for every raised scar or person.

04 · Questions to ask a qualified clinician

Focus on diagnosis, options and follow-up

  • Has a clinician confirmed that the raised scar is a keloid rather than another condition?
  • Which exact device and intended use are being discussed, and does the evidence match them?
  • What established alternatives and their trade-offs apply to this scar?
  • How will recurrence, pigment change, infection or additional scarring be monitored?
  • Who will provide follow-up and what changes should prompt contact?

Do not treat a keloid or other raised lesion at home. A clinician can explain whether medical assessment or another treatment pathway is appropriate.

05 · Study and limits

This is evidence appraisal, not a treatment recommendation

The study offers preliminary observations in a small, selected sample. It does not establish comparative effectiveness, long-term recurrence, safety for all skin types or suitability of the technology offered at this clinic. The clinic’s exact device and indication remain unconfirmed.
