Plasma treatment recovery: how long does it take?

“Plasma” does not identify one device or procedure. The appearance and recovery time described for one system do not establish what to expect from another.

First, identify the technology being proposed

The local service page describes a superficial plasma technology and says preparation, parameters and recovery vary. It does not publicly identify the device manufacturer and model. Without that information, no reliable number of days for redness, flaking or time away from activities can be given, and we cannot say whether spots or crusts should occur.

This matters because plasma exeresis and plasma skin-regeneration systems may use different devices, purposes and effects. Information about one should not be copied to another just because both use the word “plasma.”

One clinical source shows how device protocols change recovery

Two small clinical studies of the Portrait PSR system illustrate how recovery varied by protocol. In a low-energy study of eight volunteers receiving three sessions, investigators observed complete re-epithelialization in four days and average redness lasting six days. In a two-site prospective study of one high-energy PSR3 session, epidermal loss occurred over the following 24–48 hours and recovery took about seven days. These are findings for specific protocols and study samples, published in 2007 — not a prediction for the local service.

Those observations do not predict recovery for the Londrina service: its page does not confirm use of Portrait or publish equivalent settings or indications. DermNet is also a clinical overview of an identified device, not the instructions for whichever device may be offered here.

Confirm aftercare and contact details before treatment

  • Exact manufacturer, model and plasma modality; ask how the proposal relates to the device instructions for use.
  • What skin appearance is expected for that area and modality — and which changes should not be assumed “normal” without assessment.
  • Written instructions that apply to cleansing, products, makeup, sun, exercise and other procedures in the area.
  • When the team plans to review healing and how to contact them during and outside appointment hours.
  • What to do if pain increases, the area worsens, there is discharge, extensive blistering, fever or a major color change.

Do not pick crusts or apply acids, retinoids, antiseptics, antibiotics or home remedies to healing skin unless the responsible team instructs you to. Guidance should match the device, area and procedure actually performed.

When to seek help

The service page advises reporting intense pain, discharge, progressive worsening, fever, extensive blisters or a major color change; some of these signs may require medical assessment. If something is worsening or concerning, contact the responsible team instead of waiting for a generic timeline found online. Seek urgent care for an emergency.

The NHS guide to wounds after dermatological surgery also lists increasing redness, heat, pain, discharge and fever as signs to contact health professionals about. It is general surgical-wound guidance, not a list of expected plasma effects or a plasma aftercare protocol.

References and limits

  • Clinical study of low-energy Portrait plasma skin regeneration — eight volunteers, three sessions and recovery follow-up; it does not identify the local platform.
  • Pilot study of Portrait PSR3 for facial rejuvenation — a two-site prospective study; the device and protocol differ from the unidentified local service.
  • Cambridge University Hospitals NHS: advice after skin surgery — general warning signs for wounds, not a plasma aftercare manual.

The clinic’s device, protocol, recovery timeline and contact process must be confirmed by the practitioner before treatment.
