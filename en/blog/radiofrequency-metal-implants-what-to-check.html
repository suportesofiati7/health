Radiofrequency and metal implants: what should you check?

Plates, screws, prostheses and other metal implants do not have one universal answer for aesthetic radiofrequency. Learn what to disclose and which instructions need checking.

“Metal in the body” does not describe one situation

A surgical plate and screws, a joint prosthesis, a wire, a dental implant and an implanted electronic device differ in material, location and purpose. Aesthetic radiofrequency also comes in different systems, applicators and delivery modes; they are not interchangeable.

So a blanket answer such as “all metal is prohibited” or “metal makes no difference” is not reliable. Instructions for one specific device do not establish safety for another. The clinic does not publicly identify its radiofrequency model; the exact model and its instructions need to be confirmed before a decision.

What public guidance can—and cannot—tell you

For radiofrequency body-contouring devices, the FDA advises against use in people with metal under the skin from an implant or injury. Its scope is the procedure category described by the agency in the United States; the guidance does not identify this clinic’s device, replace the exact model manual or constitute a Brazilian regulation. The FDA also advises telling the provider about prostheses, metal implants and screws.

Instructions vary by product. For example, Cutera publishes device-specific restrictions for truSculpt and Secret RF, including warnings related to implants. INDIBA publishes different guidance for its systems. These differences show why a contraindication or compatibility claim should not be transferred between brands, frequencies and applicators.

Also, being a passive rather than electronic implant does not automatically prove compatibility. Its location relative to the treatment area, the device type and current instructions still matter.

Information to bring to an assessment

  • What type of implant or material do you have, and where is it located?
  • When was it placed? Do you have an implant card, surgical report or manufacturer name?
  • Would the planned treatment area be over or near the implant?
  • What are the exact brand and model of the device and applicator? Do current instructions allow or restrict treatment in this situation?
  • Should the team caring for the implant or its manufacturer be consulted before booking?

You do not need to remove, manipulate or change an implant yourself. If the device-specific documentation does not clarify the situation, the decision should wait for professional review.

What this page cannot determine

The sources cited here do not confirm the device used locally, establish a universal safe distance between an applicator and an implant, or decide whether a particular person can have radiofrequency. They also do not override instructions from the implant manufacturer or surgical team.

Tell the practitioner about every implanted device or material, even if it is far from the area you wish to treat. The practitioner can then check the appropriate documentation and explain whether there is enough information to proceed, postpone or consider another option.

Sources and limitations

  • FDA: non-invasive body-contouring technologies and situations to discuss before radiofrequency use.
  • Cutera: truSculpt-specific instructions list restrictions involving metal implants (check the applicable model and version).
  • Cutera: Secret RF safety information.
  • INDIBA: manufacturer information about its systems and metal implants.
