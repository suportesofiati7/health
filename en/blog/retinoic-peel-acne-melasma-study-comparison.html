Retinoic peels: do acne and melasma studies answer the same question?

Two trials assessed retinoic peels for different conditions, using different formulas and outcomes. Learn why an acne result does not automatically transfer to melasma—or to a local protocol.

Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Educational study appraisal; it does not recommend a peel or establish the local protocol.

Inflammatory acne and melasma are not the same outcome

One study may measure inflammatory acne lesions; another may assess the area and severity of pigmentation and melasma-related quality of life. Even when both use a peel called “retinoic,” diagnosis, concentration, vehicle, comparator and follow-up period change how the findings should be read.

The acne trial studied 30 people

A 2024 trial included 30 people with different degrees of inflammatory acne. Across six sessions every two weeks, it compared a 5% retinoic-acid peel on one side of the face with 30% salicylic acid on the other. The authors reported improvement on both sides and an advantage for retinoic acid in later sessions. The small sample and limited follow-up do not establish a general recommendation or a result for a formula used elsewhere.

The melasma trial tested a different formulation

A Brazilian trial randomized 60 women with melasma into three groups of 20: conventional 1% retinoic acid, 1% retinoic acid in a microemulsion, or placebo. Participants received four sessions 15 days apart, with assessments through day 60. The study included Fitzpatrick phototypes I–IV and excluded V–VI. The authors reported different average reductions in the pigmentation outcome, with a larger reduction in the microemulsion group. It did not assess acne, test a 5% peel or establish results for phototypes or formulas it did not study.

How to read the studies without merging conclusions

The trials cannot be directly compared: they studied different conditions, formulas, comparators and measures. Ask which concern and diagnosis are being considered, which goal will be followed, what product or formulation is under discussion, and what alternatives and limits apply. Do not choose a concentration, schedule or combination from a paper. Care and protocol depend on professional assessment.

Studies and limitations

These are small studies with different formulas and clinical questions. They do not identify a local product, indication or protocol, or predict an individual outcome.
