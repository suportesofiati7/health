Does melasma return after fading? Understanding maintenance

A practical guide to recurrence, maintenance and questions to bring to an individual pigment assessment.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Evidence-informed reading

This is an educational guide, not a diagnosis or a prescription. It does not define which product, medicine or procedure Franciele uses.

01 · Fading is not the same as removing the tendency

Why improvement may still need follow-up

Melasma can persist for years and return after it fades. A Latin American consensus describes the condition as chronic and relapsing; recurrence alone does not prove that the previous plan was useless or that a person “did something wrong.” Assessment should consider whether pigment returned in the same pattern, whether there is irritation or relevant exposure, and whether the appearance still matches the earlier diagnosis.

02 · Maintenance is part of a plan, not a universal prescription

What may continue after improvement

The Latin American consensus recommends continued photoprotection and considering ongoing topical therapy after clinical remission. This does not mean using the same ingredient, product or frequency indefinitely. Choice depends on diagnosis, irritation history, treatments used and factors such as pregnancy, breastfeeding or medicines. Do not restart a lightening agent, increase frequency or extend use on your own; ask for a clear goal and review point.

03 · Photoprotection has to fit light exposure and real life

Better questions than “what is the perfect sunscreen?”

The AAD recommends sun protection as part of melasma care and notes that sunscreens containing pigments such as iron oxide may be considered. Sun exposure and visible light can contribute to darkening for some people. A workable plan should fit routine, skin tone and tolerance; texture, coverage and reapplication are useful questions for the practitioner. No single sunscreen can guarantee that melasma will not recur.

04 · Irritation can make a dark patch harder to interpret

Do not try to compensate for recurrence with more intensity

Burning, peeling and inflammation can make skin uncomfortable and change its appearance. More products or procedures do not automatically mean better control: the Latin American consensus notes that irritating interventions and unprotected exposure may be associated with relapse. If skin worsened, became sensitive or changed color after an ingredient or procedure, do not escalate treatments; seek guidance. This page cannot diagnose dermatitis or recommend a medicine.

05 · A new or different spot needs a fresh assessment

Not every color change is melasma returning

If an area changes shape, color or behavior, or a lesion appears that differs from usual patches, do not assume it is melasma. Clinical assessment can confirm whether the diagnosis remains appropriate or whether another condition should be investigated before pigment treatment. Our guide to changing spots explains why lightening first can be the wrong next step.

06 · Procedures may be one part, not the whole plan

What the evidence lets us say about peels and light

The AAD describes peels, microneedling and laser/light technologies as options a dermatologist may consider in some plans, together with topical treatment and sun protection. This does not mean every technology is suitable for every case of melasma, or identify which modalities or products the clinic’s professional protocol uses. Ask about the diagnosis, the purpose of a proposed procedure, irritation or pigment risks, alternatives and the maintenance plan.

07 · Bring concrete questions to consultation

A control plan should account for what happens next

What diagnosis guides treatment? How will we know improvement is sufficient? What continues in my routine after this phase, and when will we review it? How should the plan change if irritation appears or the pigment returns? What protection is realistic for my routine? Which changes call for dermatology assessment? The answer may involve ongoing care, a plan review or another referral—not necessarily another procedure.
