Microneedling: which medicines and health conditions should you disclose?

Medicines, health conditions and healing history can change the questions asked in a consultation. Learn what to share without deciding on your own whether a procedure is suitable.

By Franciele Sofiati · CRBM 6277Evidence-informed reading

This guide helps you prepare for a conversation; it does not diagnose, define universal contraindications or replace assessment by the responsible practitioner. Do not stop, delay or change a prescribed medicine to try to qualify for a procedure.

01 · Bring a complete list

Medicines, supplements and recent treatments can matter too

Share current and recent medicines, supplements, products applied to the skin, allergies and treatments you have had or planned. If possible, note the name, dose and when you started or stopped each one—without stopping anything to create the list. This gives the practitioner context and, when needed, a reason to ask the prescriber for advice or recommend medical assessment.

02 · Some medicines call for a specific discussion

Isotretinoin and medicines that affect bleeding do not have one online rule

The FDA lists current isotretinoin use and use in the previous six months among situations to discuss for microneedling devices evaluated in the United States. This is not a universal Brazilian rule and does not replace the instructions for the actual device; sources and procedures can differ. Also disclose anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines and any medicine associated with bleeding, and do not stop them on your own. The responsible practitioner should assess your situation and consult the prescriber when needed.

03 · Share conditions that affect skin or healing

Immune health, diabetes, skin conditions and prior scars need context

Tell the practitioner about conditions affecting immunity, healing or blood-sugar control; eczema, psoriasis or another active condition in the area; a tendency to raised scars or keloids; and recent procedures or treatments. These details do not automatically mean a person can or cannot have microneedling. A decision depends on the current condition, area, device, purpose and appropriate clinical assessment.

04 · Allergies and anesthetics

Report past reactions, even if they happened during another procedure

Share allergies or sensitivities to topical or local anesthetics, metals, adhesives, antiseptics and skin products. If you have had hives, swelling, trouble breathing or another significant reaction, describe what happened and when. Do not test a new anesthetic or product at home to prepare for the consultation.

05 · Mention pregnancy and other ongoing care

The full context can help determine the next step

Disclose pregnancy, breastfeeding, cancer treatment or immunosuppressive treatment, a recent infection and any health change since your last consultation. The FDA includes some of these situations in guidance for devices authorized in the United States; that list does not by itself establish local practice. If you are receiving medical care, share the information with the practitioner and follow appropriate clinical advice.

06 · Questions to bring

A useful conversation connects your history to the actual plan

Which device and purpose are being considered? Do the manufacturer’s instructions address my history? Should I speak with the professional who prescribed a medicine? Will a product or anesthetic be used, and how will allergies be considered? What alternative or delay makes sense if assessment raises a concern? Ask for answers that refer to the device and protocol actually being considered; a generic list cannot determine your eligibility.
