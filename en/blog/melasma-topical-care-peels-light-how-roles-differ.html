Melasma: how do topical care, peels and light compare?

Photoprotection, skin-applied care and procedures may each have a role, but they are not interchangeable steps. Choice depends on diagnosis, history and professional assessment.

Confirm the condition before comparing techniques

Brown marks can have different causes and patterns. Melasma is not a synonym for every form of hyperpigmentation, and a post-inflammatory mark or a changing pigmented lesion may call for a different assessment. This guide discusses decisions considered for assessed melasma; it cannot diagnose a mark from a photograph or description.

The 2024 Latin American consensus says management should account for diagnosis, severity, impact, triggers, previous treatments, skin sensitivity and pregnancy status. If the cause of a mark is unclear, first ask who should assess it before any procedure is considered.

Photoprotection and topical care are continuing parts of the plan

The regional consensus considers photoprotection essential to prevent, treat and reduce melasma relapse. It also describes topical agents as a prevailing component of management, selected according to evidence, availability, safety, tolerability and personal characteristics. This does not mean one product suits everyone or that you should start or combine active ingredients without guidance.

Ask the practitioner or dermatologist what goal is expected from each skin-applied treatment, how tolerability will be reviewed, and what maintenance is being considered. Share irritation, allergies, products and medicines; do not stop prescribed treatment on your own.

Peels and devices are possible additions, not equivalent treatments

The Latin American consensus lists chemical peels, microneedling, intradermal injections, lasers, light and other interventions among options that may be considered. It frames procedures as complementary to topical and oral treatment, and notes that combinations and outcomes need further study. A category appearing in a consensus does not show that every device, depth, substance or protocol works for every melasma pattern.

Procedures that irritate or inflame the skin may also worsen pigmentation in some circumstances. Ask why a specific modality is proposed, which risks matter for your history and skin phototype, what alternatives exist, and what happens if your skin does not tolerate the plan. A platform’s brand name does not identify its applicator or energy source.

Compare options with concrete questions

  • What diagnosis or working diagnosis is being addressed, and who confirmed it?
  • What roles do photoprotection, topical care and a procedure each play?
  • What evidence applies to the proposed modality and condition, and what differences limit the comparison?
  • How will response, irritation or worsening be reviewed? The consensus does not set one universal timeline for improvement.
  • What maintenance may be needed after improvement, given that melasma can relapse?
  • Which product, device, applicator and protocol are actually planned for this service?

The answer should be an individual plan that can be reviewed, not a promise of permanent clearing or an automatic sequence of procedures. Diagnosis, active ingredients and local protocols require professional confirmation.

References and limits

  • Latin American consensus on the treatment of melasma (published online in 2024; issue dated 2025). It recommends photoprotection, individualized care, topical options as a prevailing component, and complementary procedures; it does not establish this clinic’s protocol.
  • Evidence-based review of melasma treatments (2019). It notes limitations from heterogeneous designs, small samples and insufficient follow-up.
