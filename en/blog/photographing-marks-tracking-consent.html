Photographing dark marks during care: what should you compare and ask?

A photograph records appearance at one moment; it does not diagnose the cause or prove, by itself, that a plan worked. If your care team recommends documentation, compare images made consistently and agree on how they will be stored and used.

A comparison needs similar conditions

Perceived color in an image changes with lighting, distance, angle, camera exposure, white balance, makeup, filters and automatic phone processing. A dermatology methods paper describes standardizing the setting, camera, photographer, positioning and framing so clinical images can be compared more accurately. This is different from a casual selfie or an image prepared for promotion.

If a practitioner asks for follow-up photos, ask how to repeat the background, lighting, distance, position, expression and timing relative to products or a procedure. Avoid editing, enhancing or applying filters. A photograph can help document; it does not replace an examination, diagnosis, clinical scale or instrumental measurement when those are needed.

Standardized methods for photography in procedural dermatology (Hexsel et al., 2017; PubMed).

What a photograph can and cannot answer

Consistent images can support a conversation about visible change over time. Still, a visual difference does not automatically identify whether a mark was melasma, post-inflammatory pigmentation or another condition; it does not prove which part of a plan caused the difference; and it cannot predict another person’s result. A mark that appears, changes shape or color, bleeds, forms a sore or changes quickly needs appropriate assessment, not only cosmetic comparison.

When discussing progress, ask which goal is being followed, when the team expects to review it, who will assess it and which factors may change appearance. Do not increase a dose, combine products or add a procedure because a photo looks unchanged or darker.

Agree on privacy and purpose before sharing images

Before sharing a photo, confirm which secure channel to use, who will access it and whether it is for care only or also for teaching, social media or promotion. Those are different purposes. Ask how to decline promotional use without affecting care and which storage and deletion rules apply. This clinic’s photography and consent workflow must be confirmed by the responsible team; this page does not describe a local policy.

Do not send intimate images, documents, a face or other identifying details through a channel you do not recognize. If an appointment is better suited to the assessment, ask whether that is preferred.

Useful follow-up questions

  • What is the image meant to follow: an area, perceived color or another change?
  • How should I repeat lighting, angle, distance and time to reduce camera variation?
  • Who will assess the image, and when should I choose an in-person visit instead?
  • Which channel should I use, who can see the image and how long will it be kept?
  • Are clinical records and promotional use handled separately? How can I authorize or decline each purpose?

For a new or changing mark, seek clinical assessment rather than drawing a conclusion from a camera. The team should confirm its documentation and consent process before requesting images.
